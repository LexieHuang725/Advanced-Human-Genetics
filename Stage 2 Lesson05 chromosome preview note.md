**Key idea:**  
Higher resolution = smaller chromosome region = fewer genes = smaller abnormalities can be detected.

**Important:** Very small **microdeletions/microduplications (CNVs)** are usually better detected by **chromosomal microarray**, not routine karyotype banding.

3. What are the global clinical features of an individual with a chromosomal
condition?

* Developmental delay / intellectual disability
* Growth abnormalities- prenatal growth restriction
- short stature
- sometimes abnormal overgrowth

* Multiple congenital anomalies- cardiac defects
  - renal abnormalities
  - skeletal abnormalities
  - CNS abnormalities

* Characteristic dysmorphic features
  * hypotonia(floppy baby)
  * Reproductive abnormalities / infertility
  * Sometimes behavioral or neurologic abnormalities

> Developmental delay + dysmorphism + multiple congenital anomalies → think chromosome abnormality.

### Evolution of Cytogenetics
| Era | Main advance | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Dark Ages**(befre 1950) | Chromosome number/structure poorly understood; chromosomes difficult to visualize | Limited clinical diagnosis (48 chromosomes) |
| **2. Hypotonic Period**((1951–1956) | Tao-chiuh Hsu, hypotonic solution swelled cells and spread chromosomes apart | 48 -> 46 chromosomes |
| **3. Trisomy Period** (1959–Early 1970s) | Chromosome-number abnormalities linked to disease | Led to recognition of **trisomy 21, trisomy 18, trisomy 13, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome** |
| **4. Banding Era** (1970s–1980s) | G-banding and other staining methods produced characteristic chromosome bands | Allowed detection of **deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations** and identification of specific chromosome regions |
| **5. Molecular Era** (1980s–Present) | FISH, chromosomal microarray, and sequencing added DNA-level resolution | Detects **microdeletions, microduplications/CNVs, specific rearrangements, and smaller genomic abnormalities** |

### Clinical Cytogenetics

1. Who are the members of the clinical cytogenetics team and what are their
qualifications?

| Team member | Typical qualification / role |
|---|---|
| **Clinical cytogeneticist / laboratory director** | Usually MD, PhD, or equivalent with specialized genetics training; interprets results and oversees the laboratory |
| **Cytogenetic technologist** | Usually bachelor's degree in biology/genetics/medical laboratory science; may have **CG(ASCP)** certification; cultures cells, prepares chromosomes, performs karyotyping/FISH |
| **Molecular/cytogenomics technologist** | Performs FISH, microarray, and other molecular chromosome tests |
| **Genetic counselor** | Master's-level genetics professional; explains testing, results, recurrence risks, and family implications |
| **Medical geneticist / ordering physician** | Evaluates the patient and determines which genetic test is appropriate |

Clinical cytogenetics laboratories are usually found in:

- **Academic medical centers / university hospitals**

- **Children's hospitals**
- **Cancer centers**
- **Commercial/reference laboratories**

They may test samples from **blood, bone marrow, amniotic fluid, chorionic villi, products of conception, tumors, and other tissues**.

### G (Giemsa banding) - Characterized by regions that stain positive for Giemsa; approximately 400-500 total bands is average.
* **Dark bands**: more highly condensed, contain more heterochromatin and are
the location of **less transcriptionally active genes** such as those expressed at
specific times during development (i.e., tissue-specific genes).
* **Light bands**: less condensed, contains more euchromatin (unique copy
DNA), and the location of **more transcriptionally active genes** involved in the
day-to-day activities of cell (i.e., housekeeping genes)

### High Resolution Banding
Uses compounds that interfere with condensation, leading to
longer chromosomes (prophase or pro-metaphase); 800-1200 total bands. This technique
is used **if a chromosome abnormality is highly suspected following a normal G-banded
karyotype.**

## Nomenclature and Banded Chromosomes (ISCN nomenclature)
* Chromosome landmark: a consistent
and distinct morphologic feature that is useful and
important in identifying a particular chromosome.

* A region: the area found between two
adjacent landmarks.

> For example, 6q23
means chromosome 6, q arm, region 2, band 3. Each major band within a region is
numbered, with band 1 nearest to centromere. Each landmark starts a new band.
Telomeres do not have numbers; referred to as pter or tel.
> four items are required: the
chromosome number, the chromosome arm designation, the region number and the
band number within that region. They are always listed in this order without spacing
or punctuation. There can be up to 9 sub-bands within a major band. A decimal is
Chromosomal Structure & Abnormalities
added to represent a sub-band

## Fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH)
Molecular probes are constructed to be
complementary (cDNA) to a specific sequence of target DNA. In the laboratory, the DNA
strands are denatured exposing the nucleotide sequence
of each strand. As the strands reanneal, the probe will recognize and
attach to its complementary target sequence. The fluorescently labeled DNA is then
visualized using a fluorescent microscope lens

## Chromosomal Microarray Analysis
**Normally use both aCGH and SNP array, but it's important to distinguish**

<img width="654" height="606" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/19de7b64-f3f3-4c43-99f0-cfc01a601148" />

### Array Comparative Genomic Hybridization (aCGH)
**deletion, duplication, CNV, have control vs. no control, CANNOT DETERMINE TRIPLOIDY**

- Detects **copy number variants (CNVs)**: chromosomal **deletions and duplications**, including some whole-chromosome gains such as trisomy.
- Has a **higher resolution and detection rate than routine karyotyping** for submicroscopic imbalances.
- Patient DNA and reference DNA are labeled with different fluorescent dyes and hybridized to the same array.
- **Higher patient signal = duplication**; **lower patient signal = deletion**.
- Useful when a patient has **developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, congenital anomalies, or dysmorphic features** with a normal karyotype.
- **Cannot detect balanced translocations or inversions**, because there is no net gain or loss of DNA.
- Traditional aCGH also does **not reliably detect triploidy**.

**Look at small reads, at specific point mutation**
- Uses known **single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)** across the genome to evaluate both **copy number** and **allele pattern**.
- Can detect:
  - **Deletions and duplications**
  - **Regions of homozygosity**
  - **Uniparental disomy (UPD)**
  - **Triploidy**
  - Evidence of **consanguinity**
- SNP arrays can distinguish **heterozygous vs. homozygous regions**, which aCGH alone cannot.

### aCGH vs. SNP Array

| Feature | aCGH | SNP Array |
|---|---|---|
| Deletions/duplications | Yes | Yes |
| Aneuploidy | Yes | Yes |
| Balanced translocation | No | No |
| Triploidy | Usually no | Yes |
| UPD | No | Yes |
| Regions of homozygosity / consanguinity | No | Yes |

### Targeted vs. Whole-Genome Arrays
- **Targeted array:** focuses on known clinically important CNVs and reduces the chance of finding uncertain results.
- **Whole-genome array:** surveys the genome more broadly and detects more abnormalities, but is more likely to identify a **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**.

### Key Clinical Point
**Chromosomal microarray is best for detecting submicroscopic deletions and duplications that may be missed by a standard karyotype.**

| Test | What it looks for | Best clinical use | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FISH** | A **specific DNA/chromosome region** using a fluorescent probe | When you already suspect a particular abnormality (Suspected 22q11.2 deletion <br> Rapid testing for trisomy 13, 18, or 21 <br> Detecting specific rearrangements in cancer, e.g. BCR::ABL1 <br> Confirming an abnormality already found by another test) | Only examines the region(s) you probe |
| **Chromosomal microarray (CMA)** | **Genome-wide copy-number changes** | Unexplained developmental delay, ID, autism, multiple congenital anomalies, dysmorphic features | Usually cannot detect balanced translocations/inversions |

## Uniparental Disomy: 
occurs when a person inherits both copies of a chromosome (or part of a chromosome) from one parent and no copy from the other parent, instead of the normal inheritance of one copy from each parent.

### Types of Uniparental Disomy (eg. have only mom as CF carrier, but id also gets CF, usually se FISH bc already know where it went wrong)

* Isodisomy: Both copies come from a single chromosome of one parent that has been duplicated (usually due to a meiosis II error or monosomy rescue). - meiosis II
* Heterodisomy: Both different homologous chromosomes of a pair are inherited from the same parent (usually due to a meiosis I error or trisomy rescue). - meiosis I

## Copy Number Variants (CNVs)

- **CNVs** are deletions or duplications of genomic DNA that change the number of copies of a region.
- <img width="546" height="309" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/9e6d6949-2dae-4348-b39f-47eecb709f5a" />

- Many CNVs are **polymorphic** and may have **no clinical significance**.
- Some CNVs can affect genes or regulatory regions and may contribute to disease.
- CNVs detected by microarray may be classified as:
  - **Pathogenic**
  - **Likely pathogenic**
  - **Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**
  - **Likely benign**
  - **Benign**

## Clinical Use of Chromosomal Microarray (CMA)

CMA is commonly used as a **first-line postnatal test** for patients with:

- **Multiple congenital anomalies** without a recognizable syndrome
- **Developmental delay / intellectual disability (DD/ID)**
- **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)**

CMA may also be considered for other findings such as:

- Growth abnormalities
- Speech delay
- Other unexplained developmental concerns

## Follow-Up After a CNV Is Found
When CMA identifies a CNV, follow-up may include:

- **Parental testing** to determine whether the CNV is inherited or de novo
- **FISH or cytogenetic studies** when confirmation or structural clarification is needed
- **Clinical genetics evaluation**
- **Genetic counseling** to discuss significance, recurrence risk, and family implications
