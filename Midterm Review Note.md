# Exam 1 Content Outline
## Genetic principles to know (20%):
|Term|explanation|example|
|----|-----------|-------|
|Pleiotropy|A single gene mutation causes multiple, seemingly unrelated physical traits or affect multiple organ systems.|CHARGE syndrome(CHD7)|
|Anticipation|a genetic trait or disease become more severe or has an earlier age of onset to next generation| Huntington's disease(HTT), Fragile X syndrome(FMR1)|
|Parent-of-origin bias|The expression of a disease phenotype depends strictly on which parent transmitted the gene(often caused by gene imprinting)|Prader-Willi syndrome(paternal) vs. Angelman syndrome(maternal)|
|New mutation (de novo)|An alteration in a gene that exists for the first time in a family member because of mutation in germ cell(egg/sperm)|Achondroplasia(FGFR3)|
|Late/delayed age of onset|A genetic condition where symptoms don't manifest until later in life|hereditary breast cancer, Huntington disease|
|Allelic heterogeneity|Different mutations on the same gene causes same/different phenotype|Cystic Fibrosis(CFTR) hundreds different mutations all cause CF/BRCA1-caused breast/ovarian cancer|
|Locus heterogeneity|Mutations in different gene locations cause same phenotype|Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease(CMT1A)-can be AD,AR,X-linked|
|Gain-of-function/Loss-of-function mutations|gain-of-function: mutation gives resulting protein an enhanced or new/abnormal activity, typically in AD manner; Loss of function: mutation gives resulting reduction/loss of function, often AR|gain of function: achondroplasia(FGFR3)/loss of function: DMD/PHTS(PTEN, tumor suppressor gene)|
|Penetrance/nonpenetrance|The proportion of individuals expressing clinical symptoms among those with the genotype.|Marfan syndrome(FBN1)/Lynch syndrome(mismatch repair gene)|
|Variable expressivity|variation in severity and rang of clinical features among individuals with same genetic mutation|neurofibromatosis type 1(NF1)|
|Compound heterozygote|An individual has two different mutation alleles at same gene locus|/|
|Double heterozygote|An individual who is heterozygous for mutation in two separate gene loci, which gene is associated with the same disease|/|
|Consanguinity|Two individuals who are closely related by blood has reproductive relationship|/|
|True negative result|A genetic test result where a person is found not to carry a PV found in affected family member|/|
|Uninformative negative result|A negative test result in an individual where no variant was found in family yet|/|
|X-inactivation|Random process in female mammalian embryos where one X chromosome is silenced and compacted into a Barr Body|X-linked disease|

| Result | Example | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **True negative** | A sister has a confirmed BRCA2 pathogenic variant. The patient tests negative for that exact variant. | The patient did **not inherit the known familial variant**. Risk attributable to it is excluded, but ordinary cancer risk and other risk factors remain. |
| **Uninformative negative** | Several relatives have early breast cancer, but no familial pathogenic variant has been identified. The patient’s panel is negative. | The test found no explanatory variant, but **does not exclude hereditary susceptibility**. Risk assessment still uses personal and family history. |
| **VUS** | Testing identifies a PTEN variant whose significance is uncertain. | It does not establish a hereditary cancer diagnosis and should not determine management as though it were pathogenic. |

* When/how to use Hardy Weinberg - For two alleles, p + q = 1 and p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1. AA frequency = p^2; Aa = 2pq; aa = q^2. For a fully
penetrant AR disorder, incidence can equal q^2 under the model. Conditions include random mating, large
population, and no material selection, migration, or mutation effects.
Example: AR disease incidence 1/10,000. q = 0.01; p = 0.99; carrier frequency 2pq = 0.0198, about
1/50.5. Approximation 2q = 0.02 is close. Do not call q itself the carrier frequency.

Chance for disease based on allele/genotype/phenotype frequencies

* When/how to use COR/COI formulas
  For pedigree problems, COR and COI answer two different questions:
- COR = coefficient of relationship → “How genetically related are these two people?”
- COI = coefficient of inbreeding → “For one person, what is the chance that the two copies of a gene came from the same ancestor?”
The formulas are related, which is why they are easy to mix up.
1. COR: coefficient of relationship
Use COR when the question asks something like:
How related are these two relatives?
What proportion of genes do they share because of common ancestry?

For one path through a common ancestor:
\[
COR = \left(\frac12\right)^n
\]
where n = total number of parent-child links connecting the two people through that common ancestor.

COI: coefficient of inbreeding
Use COI when you're looking at the child of related parents.
The question is:
What is the probability that this child receives two alleles that are identical by descent from the same ancestor?

A very useful shortcut is:
\[
\boxed{COI_{\text{child}}=\frac12(COR_{\text{parents}})}
\]

* Chance for disease based on degree of relatives involved in consanguineous mating
For allele frequency q and inbreeding F, P(aa) = q^2 + Fpq under the population model. F is a probability
of identity by descent, not the probability of a particular disease. If a known familial allele is being traced, a
pedigree-specific transmission calculation may be more informative than a generic F formula.
