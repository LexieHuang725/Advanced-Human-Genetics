# Lesson03 - Genetic counseling for the dystrophinopathies—Practice resource of the National Society of Genetic Counselors
## Detailed summary

**Article:** Pickart et al., “Genetic counseling for the dystrophinopathies—Practice resource of the National Society of Genetic Counselors”
**Journal:** *Journal of Genetic Counseling*, 2025;34:e1892
**DOI:** 10.1002/jgc4.1892
**Nature of article:** Clinical practice resource, not a primary research study or formal systematic guideline.

### Central purpose

This paper provides genetic counselors and other healthcare professionals with a comprehensive framework for counseling people affected by, or at risk for, **dystrophinopathies** caused by pathogenic variants in the **DMD gene**.

The dystrophinopathy spectrum includes:

* Duchenne muscular dystrophy, or DMD
* Becker muscular dystrophy, or BMD
* Intermediate phenotypes
* Dystrophin-associated dilated cardiomyopathy
* Symptomatic or subclinically affected heterozygous XX individuals

The central message is that dystrophinopathies should no longer be presented as a simple division between “severe Duchenne” and “mild Becker.” They form a broad clinical spectrum with substantial variation between individuals—even among relatives carrying the same variant.

Modern molecular testing has improved diagnosis, but it has also uncovered variants whose clinical consequences are difficult to predict. At the same time, treatments increasingly depend on the exact molecular variant, making accurate testing and careful interpretation more important than ever.

---

## 1. How the resource was developed

The document was produced by a multicenter group of board-certified genetic counselors with experience in:

* Clinical management of dystrophinopathies
* Neuromuscular genetics
* Laboratory analysis of the DMD gene
* Reproductive genetic counseling
* Advocacy and patient support

Its conclusions are based on professional experience, relevant medical literature, and published expert-consensus recommendations.

The authors emphasize that this is a **voluntary practice resource**. It does not establish one mandatory counseling approach and does not replace individualized clinical judgment.

---

# 2. Biological basis of dystrophinopathies

## The DMD gene and dystrophin

The **DMD gene** is located at **Xp21.2–p21.1**. It is one of the largest human genes:

* More than 2 megabases in size
* Contains 79 exons
* Produces several different dystrophin isoforms

Dystrophin is part of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex. It connects the internal actin cytoskeleton of the muscle cell to proteins associated with the cell membrane and extracellular matrix.

Its major function is to stabilize muscle fibers during contraction. When dystrophin is absent or reduced:

1. Muscle contraction places abnormal mechanical stress on the membrane.
2. The muscle-fiber membrane becomes vulnerable to damage.
3. Repeated injury leads to muscle-fiber degeneration.
4. Muscle tissue is progressively replaced by fibrotic and fatty tissue.
5. Skeletal, respiratory, and cardiac muscles progressively lose function.

The brain also expresses dystrophin isoforms, explaining why some individuals have cognitive, behavioral, language, or neurodevelopmental manifestations.

## Important isoforms

The paper discusses three full-length isoforms:

* **Dp427m:** primarily expressed in skeletal and cardiac muscle
* **Dp427b:** expressed in cortical neurons and the hippocampus
* **Dp427p:** associated with cerebellar Purkinje cells and low-level skeletal-muscle expression

Shorter isoforms include **Dp140** and **Dp71**. Variants affecting these brain-expressed isoforms, particularly variants involving the region encoding Dp71, are associated with a greater likelihood of:

* Cognitive impairment
* Autism spectrum disorder
* ADHD
* Language and learning difficulties

Therefore, the position of a variant within the gene may influence manifestations beyond muscle weakness.

# 3. Clinical spectrum

## Duchenne muscular dystrophy

DMD is generally the more severe phenotype.

### Early presentation

Symptoms are noticed at an average age of approximately 2.6 years, while the average diagnosis occurs around 4.1 years. Early findings may include:

* Delayed gross-motor milestones
* Difficulty running or climbing stairs
* Frequent falls
* Toe walking
* Waddling gait
* Proximal muscle weakness
* Calf pseudohypertrophy
* Positive Gower sign

A **Gower sign** occurs when a child uses the hands to “walk” up the legs while rising from the floor because the hip and thigh muscles are weak.

**Calf pseudohypertrophy** means that the calves appear enlarged, but the enlargement reflects replacement of muscle with fat and fibrotic tissue rather than increased functional muscle.

Serum creatine kinase, or CK, is usually extremely elevated—sometimes approximately 100 times the upper limit of normal.

### Disease progression

A child with DMD may initially gain motor abilities because normal development temporarily compensates for muscle degeneration. Around age seven, many children enter a plateau phase. Afterward, muscle degeneration overtakes normal developmental gains.

Without corticosteroid treatment, loss of independent ambulation historically occurs around 10–12 years. Corticosteroids may delay this by approximately one to two years, with longer-term studies showing delays in multiple mobility milestones.

### Multisystem manifestations

DMD affects more than limb muscles.

**Respiratory manifestations include:**

* Restrictive lung disease
* Weak cough and difficulty clearing secretions
* Recurrent pneumonia
* Hypoventilation
* Respiratory failure

**Orthopedic manifestations include:**

* Contractures
* Osteoporosis
* Scoliosis
* Fracture risk

**Cardiac manifestations include:**

* Left-ventricular fibrosis
* Ventricular dilation
* Reduced ejection fraction
* Dilated cardiomyopathy
* Heart failure
* Arrhythmias

Approximately one-quarter of individuals with DMD develop dilated cardiomyopathy during the first decade, and more than 90% of adults with DMD show some left-ventricular involvement.

Importantly, cardiac disease may be severe even when a nonambulatory person reports few symptoms. Limited physical activity can mask typical symptoms of heart failure.

### Neurodevelopmental manifestations

DMD is also associated with:

* Intellectual disability
* Learning disabilities
* Language difficulties
* ADHD
* Autism spectrum disorder
* Anxiety
* Obsessive-compulsive symptoms

One cited study found a mean full-scale IQ of approximately 84, with close to 30% of participants having a full-scale IQ below 70. These findings are variable and do not occur in everyone.

### Survival

Among individuals with DMD born after 1990, the paper reports a median age at death of approximately 28.1 years. Survival continues to improve because of earlier cardiac treatment, respiratory support, corticosteroids, and coordinated multidisciplinary care.

---

## Becker muscular dystrophy

BMD generally involves:

* Later onset
* Slower progression
* Greater clinical variability
* Greater residual dystrophin production

Classic BMD may present around ages 10–13 with:

* Hip-girdle and thigh weakness
* Exercise-related cramping
* Difficulty climbing stairs
* Trouble keeping up with peers
* Calf pseudohypertrophy
* Elevated CK
* Occasional gross-motor delay

Individuals with classic BMD generally remain ambulatory beyond age 16. Some people develop symptoms only in adulthood, and others remain independently ambulatory throughout life.

The BMD spectrum can include:

* Mild or subclinical muscle weakness
* Isolated hyperCKemia
* Muscle pain and cramps
* Rhabdomyolysis or myoglobinuria
* Dilated cardiomyopathy with little skeletal-muscle involvement
* Cognitive or behavioral manifestations
* Autism-related features

Cardiomyopathy may be out of proportion to skeletal-muscle weakness. Consequently, apparently mild muscle disease does not necessarily mean low cardiac risk.

---

## X-linked dilated cardiomyopathy

Some DMD variants primarily cause cardiac disease. Hemizygous XY individuals may develop early-onset dilated cardiomyopathy without clinically obvious skeletal-muscle weakness.

Features include:

* Onset often in the second or third decade
* Possible elevation of CK
* Heart failure
* Ventricular arrhythmias
* Potentially severe or fatal cardiac disease

Heterozygous XX relatives may develop later and milder cardiac manifestations. Distinguishing isolated X-linked cardiomyopathy from very mild BMD can be difficult.

---

# 4. Heterozygous XX individuals

The article deliberately moves away from treating heterozygous XX individuals as “unaffected carriers.” Many experience cardiac, neuromuscular, or subclinical manifestations.

## Cardiac risks

All heterozygous XX individuals with a pathogenic DMD variant are considered at increased risk for:

* Dilated cardiomyopathy
* Myocardial fibrosis
* Reduced ejection fraction
* ECG abnormalities
* Peripartum cardiomyopathy

Studies cited in the paper found:

* Cardiac MRI abnormalities in as many as 49%
* Echocardiographic abnormalities in approximately 62% in one cohort
* One or more ECG abnormalities in more than 70%
* Unequivocal dilated cardiomyopathy in an estimated 7%–17%

The prevalence varies because studies use different definitions and testing methods. Cardiac MRI can detect fibrosis that might not be apparent by echocardiography.

## Neuromuscular manifestations

Possible manifestations include:

* Muscle weakness, sometimes asymmetric
* Fatigue
* Muscle pain
* Cramping
* Calf hypertrophy
* Elevated CK
* Increased muscle fat on MRI

One cited study found objectively reduced muscle strength in 40% of heterozygous participants. Approximately 81% had either reduced strength or increased muscle fat fraction.

CK is not a reliable way to determine carrier status because:

* Some carriers have normal CK.
* Levels vary over time.
* CK may decrease with age.
* Elevated CK is not specific to dystrophinopathy.

## Why severity varies

Possible mechanisms include:

* Skewed X-chromosome inactivation
* The nature and location of the DMD variant
* Modifier genes
* Turner syndrome
* Structural X-chromosome abnormalities
* Rare biallelic DMD variants

Older literature used “manifesting carrier” mainly for severely affected XX individuals. The authors suggest that this terminology may underestimate milder and subclinical manifestations.

# 5. Inheritance and recurrence risks

The condition is best described as **X-linked**, rather than strictly X-linked recessive, because heterozygous XX individuals can have clinically meaningful disease.

## If one XX parent is heterozygous

For every pregnancy, regardless of previous outcomes:

| Possible child                       | Probability |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------: |
| Hemizygous XY child with the variant |         25% |
| XY child without the variant         |         25% |
| Heterozygous XX child                |         25% |
| XX child without the variant         |         25% |

Equivalently:

* Every XY child has a 50% probability of inheriting the variant.
* Every XX child has a 50% probability of inheriting the variant.

An XX child who inherits the variant should not automatically be described as clinically unaffected because cardiac and neuromuscular manifestations are possible.

## If an XY parent has a DMD variant

An affected hemizygous XY parent transmits:

* The DMD variant to **all XX offspring**
* The DMD variant to **no XY offspring**

This occurs because an XY parent passes the X chromosome to XX offspring and the Y chromosome to XY offspring.

## De novo variants

For severe DMD:

* Approximately one-third of cases arise through a de novo variant.
* Approximately two-thirds are inherited from a heterozygous XX parent.

The probability that the mother is heterozygous is higher—reported as approximately 90%—when the affected child has a less severe phenotype.

## Germline mosaicism

A negative blood or saliva test in the mother does not reduce recurrence risk to zero. The variant may be present in some egg cells but absent from blood cells.

The paper reports:

* Older recurrence estimates of 14%–20% for transmission to any offspring
* More recent evidence of approximately a 7% risk of transmission to XY offspring

Therefore, when a child appears to have a de novo DMD variant:

* The mother should still receive counseling about germline mosaicism.
* Prenatal diagnosis may remain relevant in later pregnancies.
* At-risk sisters of the affected person may still warrant testing even if parental blood testing was negative.

This is one of the paper’s most important reproductive counseling points.

# 6. Reproductive counseling

Individuals at risk of having a child with a dystrophinopathy should ideally receive counseling and molecular testing:

* Before conception, or
* As early as possible during pregnancy

Options should be presented nondirectively and may include:

* Pregnancy without prenatal genetic testing
* Chorionic villus sampling
* Amniocentesis
* IVF with preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disease, or PGT-M
* Donor egg or donor sperm
* Adoption

## PGT-M complications

PGT-M is not always straightforward for DMD variants. Testing for deletions or duplications may require **phasing**, meaning the laboratory must determine which surrounding genetic markers travel with the pathogenic variant.

Phasing may require samples from:

* A parent
* An existing child
* Another informative relative
* Occasionally, tissue from a previous pregnancy

Without informative relatives, PGT-M may sometimes be technically impossible or unreliable, particularly for apparently de novo deletions or duplications.

The paper also mentions PGT-A with embryo-sex selection as an option that may reduce the probability of a severely affected child. However, choosing an XX embryo does not eliminate health risks because heterozygous XX individuals may develop cardiac or neuromuscular manifestations.

At the time covered by the article, routine noninvasive prenatal testing for DMD was not available in the United States. Cell-free DNA screening could be used to predict fetal sex, followed by CVS or amniocentesis when indicated, but fetal sex alone is not diagnostic.

Pregnant people carrying a DMD variant should receive cardiac evaluation because pregnancy increases circulatory workload and may expose or aggravate cardiomyopathy.

# 7. Genotype–phenotype relationships

## Types of DMD variants

The paper estimates that among people with dystrophinopathies:

* Approximately 70% have one- or multiple-exon deletions.
* Approximately 10% have exon duplications.
* Approximately 20% have small sequence variants.
* Missense variants account for only about 1%–4% of pathogenic variants.

Common deletion and duplication hotspots include:

* Exons 3–7
* Exons 45–55

## The reading-frame rule

The reading-frame rule is a useful starting point:

* **Out-of-frame variant:** usually prevents production of functional dystrophin and is more often associated with DMD.
* **In-frame variant:** usually permits production of a shorter but partially functional dystrophin and is more often associated with BMD.

An out-of-frame deletion or duplication commonly creates a premature stop codon. The abnormal transcript may then be destroyed through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, resulting in little or no dystrophin.

An in-frame variant allows translation to continue, producing a shorter dystrophin protein.

## Why this rule is not absolute

The paper strongly cautions against treating the reading-frame rule as a guarantee.

Exceptions may occur because of:

* Alternative splicing
* Exon skipping
* Alternative translation-initiation sites
* The exact genomic breakpoints
* Effects on essential dystrophin domains
* Creation of a new premature stop codon
* Differences in transcript processing
* Modifier genes
* Differences in duplication orientation and insertion location

The Leiden database suggested that approximately 9% of variants violate the expected rule. One BMD study found out-of-frame deletions or duplications in approximately 30% of its cohort.

Examples include:

* Deletion of exons 3–7 can produce BMD despite being predicted out of frame.
* An in-frame exon 3 deletion can produce DMD.
* Deletion of exons 49–51 has been reported in DMD, BMD, isolated cardiomyopathy, and asymptomatic XY individuals.
* Apparently identical duplications may produce very different outcomes depending on whether the duplicated material is inserted in tandem within DMD or elsewhere in the genome.

Therefore, a prediction such as “in frame equals Becker” is not sufficiently reliable for high-stakes reproductive counseling.

# 8. Diagnostic evaluation and testing

## Indications for evaluation

Testing may be prompted by:

* Motor delay
* Proximal muscle weakness
* Difficulty running or climbing stairs
* Toe walking
* Gower sign
* Calf pseudohypertrophy
* Elevated CK
* Unexplained elevation of AST or ALT
* Developmental delay
* Cardiomyopathy
* Family history of dystrophinopathy

An important clinical warning is that elevated liver enzymes in a child may originate from damaged muscle rather than primary liver disease. CK testing can help recognize the muscular source.

A CK level above approximately 2,000 IU/L strongly supports a muscular dystrophy in the appropriate setting, although CK itself is nonspecific.

## Molecular testing as the standard

Genetic testing is now the diagnostic gold standard because it is:

* Less invasive than muscle biopsy
* More comprehensive
* More accessible
* Usually more cost-effective
* Necessary for determining eligibility for variant-specific treatments

Modern next-generation sequencing assays can detect:

* Exon-level deletions
* Exon-level duplications
* Single-nucleotide variants
* Small insertions and deletions
* Some splice-altering variants
* In some laboratories, selected deep intronic variants

## Selecting the test

The testing strategy should match the clinical indication:

| Clinical situation          | Suggested approach                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Classic DMD/BMD phenotype   | Comprehensive DMD analysis using an NGS-based assay with deletion/duplication detection |
| Atypical muscular dystrophy | Multigene neuromuscular or muscular-dystrophy panel                                     |
| Isolated cardiomyopathy     | Cardiomyopathy panel that includes DMD                                                  |
| Known familial variant      | Targeted testing for that exact variant                                                 |
| Older incomplete testing    | Updated comprehensive molecular testing                                                 |

People previously tested using older methods may need retesting because older approaches could miss variants or describe them imprecisely. Updated testing may also be required for clinical-trial or treatment eligibility.

## Role of muscle biopsy

Muscle biopsy is no longer necessary for most straightforward cases. It may still be valuable when:

* Genetic results are negative despite strong clinical suspicion.
* A variant of uncertain significance is detected.
* The phenotype is intermediate or difficult to predict.
* Residual dystrophin needs to be measured.
* Treatment decisions depend on protein expression.
* RNA analysis of muscle may clarify abnormal splicing.

Dystrophin can be evaluated through:

* Immunohistochemistry
* Western blot
* RNA sequencing or transcript analysis

# 9. Interpreting uncertain or negative results

Even comprehensive testing has limitations. Approximately 2% or fewer of clinically diagnosed DMD/BMD cases may remain without an identified pathogenic DMD variant.

Possible explanations include:

* Deep intronic variants
* Structural variants outside routinely analyzed regions
* Complex rearrangements
* Variants not reported by the laboratory
* Difficult-to-interpret splice variants
* Another neuromuscular disorder that mimics dystrophinopathy

Useful follow-up approaches include:

* Updated or broader molecular testing
* Multigene panels
* Exome or genome sequencing
* Muscle biopsy
* RNA sequencing
* Segregation analysis in relatives
* Clinical evaluation of relatives carrying the same variant

For a variant of uncertain significance, cosegregation with disease may support pathogenicity. Finding the variant in apparently unaffected adult XY relatives may argue against pathogenicity—but those relatives should undergo appropriate cardiac and neuromuscular assessment because mild disease may otherwise be missed.

A variant’s presence in ClinVar, HGMD, LOVD, or a publication does not automatically establish that it is pathogenic. Conversely, presence in a general-population database does not automatically establish that it is benign because DMD-related phenotypes may be mild, late-onset, sex-dependent, or incompletely penetrant.

# 10. Expanded carrier screening

Including DMD on expanded carrier-screening panels has created new counseling challenges.

People without a personal or family history may receive results labeled “pathogenic,” but the identified variant may have:

* Incomplete penetrance
* An uncertain phenotype
* Association with BMD rather than DMD
* Association only with hyperCKemia or cardiomyopathy
* Highly variable expression
* Questionable classification

Population screening appears to identify a relatively large number of in-frame deletions and duplications. Some variants initially presumed disease-causing have later been found in healthy or only mildly affected XY relatives.

The authors recommend that every person identified with a DMD variant through expanded carrier screening receive genetic counseling involving:

1. Examination of the exact variant.
2. Review of the evidence supporting classification.
3. Assessment of known genotype–phenotype associations.
4. Correlation with personal and family history.
5. Discussion of uncertainty and intrafamilial variability.
6. Consideration of testing and clinical evaluation in relatives.

The counselor should avoid automatically presenting the most severe DMD phenotype as the expected outcome when the variant has been associated with mild, variable, or absent manifestations.

---

# 11. Newborn screening

DMD newborn-screening programs generally use:

1. CK-MM measurement from a dried blood spot.
2. Confirmatory DMD genetic testing when CK-MM is elevated.

Potential advantages include:

* Diagnosis before clear symptoms develop
* Earlier specialist care
* Earlier cardiac and respiratory surveillance
* Reduced diagnostic delay
* Earlier access to treatments and clinical trials
* Earlier reproductive information for the family

Potential challenges include:

* False-positive or ambiguous CK results
* Identification of heterozygous XX newborns
* Variants with uncertain severity
* Psychosocial effects of diagnosing a progressive condition before symptoms
* Need for long-term follow-up and counseling

The paper reported that New York and Ohio had mandated DMD newborn screening at the time of publication.

# 12. Management recommendations

## DMD

Management should involve a multidisciplinary team, commonly including:

* Neuromuscular specialist
* Cardiology
* Pulmonology
* Physical and occupational therapy
* Rehabilitation
* Orthopedics
* Nutrition
* Endocrinology
* Psychology or psychiatry
* Genetics and genetic counseling
* Palliative care when appropriate

Major goals include:

* Preserving ambulation and independence
* Preventing contractures
* Monitoring bone health
* Detecting respiratory decline early
* Detecting cardiac involvement before symptoms
* Supporting cognition, behavior, education, and mental health

Cardiac and pulmonary surveillance must be proactive because waiting for symptoms may permit irreversible disease progression.

## BMD

No separate comprehensive consensus-care guideline for BMD was available when the paper was written. Management is individualized according to severity.

Relevant elements of DMD care may still apply, particularly:

* Cardiac surveillance
* Pulmonary surveillance
* Rehabilitation
* Physical and occupational therapy

Cardiac disease deserves particular attention because it may be severe despite mild skeletal-muscle symptoms.

## Heterozygous XX individuals

The paper recommends:

* Beginning cardiac surveillance by age 25
* Repeating cardiac imaging at least every five years if results are normal
* Increasing surveillance frequency if abnormalities are detected
* Using ECG plus echocardiography or cardiac MRI
* Obtaining cardiac evaluation before conception or early in pregnancy
* Referring symptomatic individuals to clinicians familiar with dystrophinopathy

Neuromuscular evaluation may include:

* Clinical examination
* CK or serial CK measurements
* Neuromuscular gene-panel testing
* Muscle MRI
* Muscle biopsy in selected cases
* Investigation for another neuromuscular condition

# 13. Treatments discussed in the paper

The treatment information represents the regulatory and clinical landscape at the time the article was written and should not be assumed to reflect current FDA labeling.

## Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids were described as the standard treatment for DMD, especially from approximately ages 5–15.

Benefits include:

* Improved muscle strength
* Slower functional decline
* Delayed loss of ambulation
* Slower respiratory decline
* Possible delay in cardiomyopathy

Important adverse effects include:

* Short stature
* Delayed puberty
* Weight gain and obesity
* Osteoporosis
* Immunosuppression
* Cataracts
* Behavioral or mood changes
* Adrenal crisis if treatment is stopped abruptly

The paper also mentions **vamorolone**, a newer corticosteroid intended to retain therapeutic benefits with a potentially improved adverse-effect profile.

## Exon-skipping therapy

At the article’s cutoff, four exon-skipping drugs were discussed:

| Drug        | Target exon |
| ----------- | ----------: |
| Eteplirsen  |          51 |
| Golodirsen  |          53 |
| Viltolarsen |          53 |
| Casimersen  |          45 |

These antisense oligonucleotides bind to pre-mRNA and cause a selected exon to be skipped during splicing. The objective is to convert an out-of-frame transcript into an in-frame transcript, allowing production of a shorter dystrophin protein.

Approximately 30% of people with DMD were estimated to be eligible for one of the available exon-skipping treatments.

Important qualifications:

* Eligibility depends on the exact variant.
* Not every deletion can be corrected by skipping one exon.
* Restoration of the reading frame does not guarantee that the shortened protein will function well.
* Treatment generally produces relatively small amounts of dystrophin.
* Available evidence suggested slowing rather than stopping disease progression.
* The drugs require weekly intravenous infusions.
* Access and insurance coverage may be difficult.

## Nonsense readthrough

Ataluren was discussed as an oral therapy intended to allow translation through a premature stop codon.

It applies only to individuals with qualifying nonsense variants, which account for approximately 10%–15% of DMD cases. At the time covered by the paper, it was available in some countries but not FDA-approved in the United States.

## Gene therapy

Because the complete DMD gene is too large for standard adeno-associated viral vectors, gene-replacement strategies use shortened **microdystrophin** or **minidystrophin** constructs.

The paper discusses delandistrogene moxeparvovec-rokl as a one-time intravenous gene therapy that had received accelerated FDA approval in June 2023 for a limited pediatric population.

Major limitations described include:

* It is not expected to be curative.
* The duration of benefit was uncertain.
* Eligibility depended on age, ambulation, variant, and antibody status.
* Some variants could increase the risk of immune reactions.
* Preexisting antibodies against the viral vector could prevent treatment.
* Serious risks, including deaths in DMD gene-therapy trials, had occurred.
* Longer-term efficacy and safety remained uncertain.

This section especially needs to be read as a historical snapshot because gene-therapy approvals, warnings, and eligibility criteria may have changed since manuscript preparation.

# 14. Clinical trials

The therapeutic pipeline includes approaches intended to:

* Replace or increase dystrophin
* Improve exon-skipping efficiency
* Reduce fibrosis
* Improve cellular energy metabolism
* Treat inflammation
* Protect muscle cells
* Improve cardiac or respiratory function

A confirmed molecular diagnosis is critical because many trials are variant-specific. Nevertheless, other experimental treatments may be applicable regardless of variant.

Counselors should clearly distinguish between:

* FDA-approved therapy
* Accelerated approval
* Investigational treatment
* Participation in clinical research

Families need realistic discussions of potential benefits, burdens, eligibility restrictions, uncertainty, and risk.

# 15. Psychosocial considerations

The article treats psychosocial management as a core component of clinical care rather than an optional supplement.

## At diagnosis

Families may experience:

* Shock
* Grief
* Fear
* Information overload
* Uncertainty about prognosis
* Difficulty understanding inheritance and recurrence risks

Counseling should be revisited over time because families absorb information gradually and their needs change as the condition progresses.

## Parental guilt

Heterozygous parents may experience guilt, shame, or remorse for transmitting the variant. The paper emphasizes that providers should address and normalize these feelings. Genetic inheritance is not a voluntary act or a parental failure.

## Communicating with children

Families should receive help deciding when and how to discuss the diagnosis with the affected child and siblings.

Conversations should be:

* Age appropriate
* Honest
* Repeated over time
* Adapted to the child’s cognitive abilities
* Supportive of identity formation and future decision-making

## Neurobehavioral needs

Reported concerns include:

* ADHD
* Intellectual disability
* Autism
* Anxiety
* Depression
* Social isolation
* Difficulty keeping up with peers

The paper cites approximate rates of:

* ADHD: 32%
* Intellectual disability: 19%–27%
* Autism spectrum disorder: 15%

These problems can affect education, adherence to treatment, family functioning, and quality of life.

## Caregiver burden

Caregivers are at risk for:

* Depression
* Stress
* Poor sleep
* Pain
* Reduced self-esteem
* Sexual dysfunction
* Impaired family functioning
* Reduced work productivity
* Physical and emotional burnout

Support should therefore extend to the entire family, not only the person with dystrophinopathy.

## Adulthood and end-of-life care

Adults may face:

* Increasing dependence in daily activities
* Loss of mobility
* Decisions about ventilation and cardiac interventions
* Concerns about relationships, independence, and employment
* Anxiety about disease progression
* End-of-life planning

Palliative care can address physical symptoms as well as emotional, social, and spiritual needs. It should not be interpreted as simply “giving up”; it can be integrated with disease-directed treatment.

# 16. Role of the genetic counselor

The paper presents genetic counselors as central members of the multidisciplinary team. Their responsibilities include:

* Explaining the diagnosis and natural history
* Reviewing the exact DMD variant
* Explaining X-linked inheritance
* Calculating recurrence risks
* Coordinating testing for relatives
* Discussing germline mosaicism
* Explaining reproductive options
* Interpreting expanded carrier-screening findings
* Discussing limitations of genotype–phenotype prediction
* Determining possible eligibility for variant-specific treatments
* Helping families understand clinical trials
* Supporting family communication
* Addressing guilt, grief, and uncertainty
* Connecting families with specialists and advocacy organizations
* Promoting equitable access to testing and treatment

The counselor’s role is particularly important because laboratory classification, predicted reading frame, clinical severity, and treatment eligibility are related—but they are not interchangeable.

# 17. Strengths and limitations of the paper

## Strengths

* Integrates clinical, molecular, reproductive, therapeutic, and psychosocial issues.
* Gives substantial attention to heterozygous XX individuals.
* Explains why genotype–phenotype predictions can fail.
* Addresses the counseling problems created by expanded carrier screening.
* Connects molecular testing directly to treatment and trial eligibility.
* Emphasizes family-centered and lifelong psychosocial care.
* Provides practical testing algorithms and points-to-consider tables.

## Limitations

* It is an expert practice resource rather than a systematic review.
* Recommendations are partly based on collective professional experience.
* Much of the evidence comes from observational studies and expert consensus.
* BMD-specific evidence and management guidelines remain limited.
* Knowledge about heterozygous XX individuals is incomplete.
* Treatment information can become outdated rapidly.
* Some prevalence and penetrance estimates vary substantially between studies.
* The terms XX and XY improve precision in some genetic explanations but do not capture every possible sex-chromosome configuration or individual identity.

---

# Overall takeaway

The paper’s most important conclusion is that **DMD-related disease is a complex, variable, multisystem spectrum—not a simple binary distinction between affected boys and unaffected female carriers**.

Three principles guide appropriate counseling:

1. **Obtain an accurate and current molecular diagnosis.**
   The exact variant affects diagnostic confidence, recurrence-risk counseling, prognosis, clinical-trial participation, and treatment eligibility.

2. **Do not overpromise genotype–phenotype prediction.**
   The reading-frame rule is useful but imperfect. The same reported variant may be associated with DMD, BMD, isolated cardiomyopathy, or minimal symptoms.

3. **Provide lifelong, family-centered care.**
   Counseling must address cardiac and neuromuscular risks in heterozygous XX individuals, reproductive choices, family testing, uncertainty, mental health, caregiver burden, and the changing treatment landscape.

In short, genetic counseling for dystrophinopathies involves much more than explaining X-linked inheritance. It requires connecting molecular biology with variable clinical expression, reproductive decision-making, treatment eligibility, psychosocial adaptation, and long-term multidisciplinary care.
